Overview

Interactive worlds such as games, AR/VR and 3D engines need sound that reacts to every action as the frames are rendered. Recent audio-visual world models generate synchronized audio, but their sound is coupled to their own video generators and cannot serve external sources such as game-engine renders or live camera feeds. Streaming video-to-audio (V2A) is a source-agnostic alternative that generates sound directly from any live pixel stream, but a usable sound engine must meet three demands at once: imperceptible latency, precise audio-visual alignment and high audio quality. Existing streaming V2A methods trade these off: shrinking the visual receptive field to cut latency degrades alignment, and causal per-patch denoising impairs audio quality.

We present LiveFoley, a plug-and-play sound engine for interactive worlds. For imperceptible latency, LiveFoley reaches a 236 ms time to first audio (0.49 RTF) on a single A800 GPU, below the 0.35 s perceptual threshold of action sounds. For precise alignment, a Trailing Sliding Window turns off-the-shelf segment-level encoders into streaming modules without retraining, matching offline models (DeSync 0.48 vs. 0.47) at a 0.08 s look-ahead. For audio quality, Future-Warmup LocDiT brings cross-patch denoising into a strictly causal backend, giving the best quality among streaming systems. In addition, a Unified Varhop Framework lets one model adapt its latency to the scene, and LiveFoley generalizes to AR/VR and game-engine footage with stable, long-form generation.

Visual frontend

Trailing Sliding Window

Segment-level alignment at frame-level latency. Anchoring the window tail to the current frame turns off-the-shelf non-causal encoders (e.g., Synchformer) into streaming modules without retraining: look-ahead drops to one hop (0.08 s) while alignment stays on par with offline models (DeSync 0.48 vs. 0.47 for MMAudio-L).

Adaptive latency

Unified Varhop Framework

One model, several latency budgets. A Synchronizer aligns tokens from different hop sizes, so a single set of weights switches between a tight hop for shooter games and a wide hop for free roaming. It matches single-hop experts in alignment and cuts visual frontend compute by up to 4×.

Audio backend

Future-Warmup LocDiT

Cross-patch denoising under strict causality. The future LM state is not yet available, but the next visual token is already buffered. Pairing it with the current hidden state pulls the upcoming patch into the denoising window, giving LiveFoley the best audio quality among streaming systems.

Method

How much delay can a listener tolerate? A lag becomes noticeable at about 0.35 s for action sounds, 0.5 s for speech and over 1 s for ambience during free roaming. Meanwhile, shrinking a chunked window to cut latency sharply hurts alignment, while our trailing sliding window does not. Segment-level alignment at frame-level latency: anchoring the window tail to the current frame decouples look-ahead from the receptive field, so delay drops to one hop with no retraining of the encoder. A Synchronizer lets one model switch among hop sizes. The future LM state is unavailable, but the next visual token is already buffered. Future-Warmup LocDiT pairs the two to pull the upcoming patch into the current denoising window, bringing cross-patch collaborative denoising into a strictly causal model.

Streaming visual frontend

In streaming, a segment-level encoder such as Synchformer can only encode a segment after it has fully arrived, so its long receptive field translates directly into look-ahead delay. The trailing sliding window anchors the window tail to the current frame: look-ahead is reduced to the hop size s (0.08, 0.16 or 0.32 s) while the full temporal context remains intact. The Unified Varhop Framework uses a Synchronizer to unify these latency modes in a single model.

Streaming visual frontend. (a) Chunked window: a smaller w lowers latency but narrows the receptive field. (b) Trailing sliding window: look-ahead drops to one hop while the full receptive field w is preserved. (c) Unified Varhop: a Synchronizer maps tokens from different hop sizes into a shared latent space.

Continuous autoregressive backend

At each 12.5 Hz step, a causal LM produces a hidden state hi, on which LocDiT denoises the current patch; standard streaming models denoise each patch in isolation. Future-Warmup LocDiT pulls the upcoming patch into the same window, conditioned on hi and the already-buffered visual token vi+1. At inference this warmup patch is discarded and regenerated with the true hi+1, so streaming causality is preserved.

Audio backend. Left: the causal LM fuses the current visual token and the LocEnc embedding of the previous patch into hi. Right: Future-Warmup LocDiT jointly denoises patches i and i+1 with the clean patch i−1 as context; patch i+1 is discarded at inference.

Results

Alignment on par with offline models at a 0.08 s look-ahead: DeSync 0.48 vs. 0.47 for the offline MMAudio-L, far ahead of V-AURA (0.69), WorldSonus (0.67) and SoundReactor (1.03). LiveFoley also has the best audio quality among streaming systems and the highest PQ of all systems. In a listening test on 16 clips, LiveFoley outperforms all online baselines on both quality (MOS-Q 3.23 vs. 2.79 / 2.75 / 3.04) and alignment (MOS-A 3.58 vs. 3.15 / 3.02 / 2.96), and approaches the offline MMAudio-L in alignment (3.79). Taking the 0.16 s mode as an example, LiveFoley reaches a TTFF of 236 ms on a single A800 GPU, below the 0.35 s perceptual threshold of action sounds. All compute stages together take under 80 ms; the rest is the algorithmic wait for one hop of video. Sustained generation runs faster than real time at an RTF of 0.49.

Listening test

Listening test on the 16 clips in the comparison below (5-point scale, 95% CI). LiveFoley leads all online systems on quality and alignment; WorldSonus runs at a comparable look-ahead (0.10 s) yet trails most clearly in alignment. Latency

Single A800, measured with a simulator that releases frames at 25 fps. The video buffer of the hop is an algorithmic wait that does not depend on hardware; all remaining stages are compute and together take under 80 ms. Sustained generation runs faster than real time at an RTF of 0.49.
